About

I'm Ava, a computer science undergraduate in the Honors College at UMass Boston, and a research intern with Dr. Farhad Nezami at Brigham and Women's Hospital, Harvard Medical School.

Most of my work comes back to one question: when can we still learn the right thing when part of the picture is hidden? Sometimes that's a latent variable quietly influencing everything in a network; sometimes it's medical data that only gets recorded when a patient has symptoms; sometimes it's a distribution that keeps drifting under your feet. I like working out exactly what is and isn't recoverable, proving the upper and lower bounds, and then testing those ideas on real data from biology and medicine.

Before computer science, I studied civil engineering at Sharif University of Technology in Tehran. I'll finish my B.S. at UMass Boston in May 2027.

Research

Learning networks with a hidden common cause Honors Thesis

2026 to 2027

with Prof. Wei Ding, UMass Boston

Suppose you watch a network of interacting units evolve over time, but there's one unobserved variable nudging every one of them. Can you still recover who's connected to whom? For sparse Ising models observed through Glauber dynamics, I show the answer is yes. The hidden variable biases things much less than you'd expect, so the amount of data needed doesn't grow with how connected it is. I also prove matching lower bounds on what the hidden coupling costs, and build a statistical test for whether such a variable is there at all.

Estimating extremes when the world keeps changing

2026 to Present

Independent project

Estimating rare tail events needs lots of data, but if the distribution drifts, old data starts to mislead you. I'm studying the best possible tradeoff between those two forces, and when averaging forecasts beats simply picking the right training window.

Learning from surgical and clinical data

2026 to Present

Nezami Lab, Brigham and Women's Hospital, Harvard Medical School

Hospitals often image a bypass graft only when something seems wrong, which makes failure risk tricky to estimate honestly. I'm studying when that risk is identifiable at all, and built an LLM pipeline to extract data on over 32,000 grafts. I also spend a lot of time stress-testing ML results in surgery, checking whether skill measures are actually reliable and catching data leakage between training and test sets.

Finding which genes were switched off

2025 to 2026

Zarringhalam Lab, UMass Boston · Clare Boothe Luce Research Fellow

In gene knockdown experiments, every perturbation also triggers a shared stress response that can hide the real signal. I designed a Bayesian model to see through that shared response, proved when it can and can't find the right regulator, and applied it to the parasite Toxoplasma gondii, where it identified the correct gene in 16 of 17 experiments.

How much of the future does a real-time classifier need?

2026

EthoLive · First place, PEAAII Applied AI Hackathon

Classifying animal behavior live means you can't peek at what happens next. Looking at mice and flies, I found that waiting just 0.1 to 0.2 seconds recovers most of the accuracy lost by going real-time, and worked out the Bayes-optimal way to use that short delay.
